Resting near Mount Fuji, there is a factory where robots build other robots in the dark. No overhead lights, no assembly line workers, running non-stop like a well-oiled machine. The only humans present are a small maintenance crew. It's ran like this for decades. The industry has coined this: the **dark factory**.

## What is a dark factory?

A dark factory, also called **lights-out manufacturing** according to wikipedia (todo: make this a link), is the practice of fully automating production so a facility can run with no human labor present on-site. The name is literally related to robots not needing need to see what they are doing, so if no person is present, you can turn the lights off. 
Japan had called it **無人工場 (mujin kojo)**, the "unmanned factory."

The idea started in the 1980s, with Roger B. Smith, CEO of General Moters attempting to the concept and partnered with Fujitsu Fanuc to create GMFanuc Robotics, a company whose mission was to build the robots that would build the cars. It actually didn't end up working. FANUC, a Japanese robotics company, finally made this dream a reality in 2001. Robots build other robots at a rate of about 50 per 24-hour shift, and the line can run unsupervised for as long as 30 days at a time. "Not only is it lights-out," says FANUC vice president Gary Zywiol, "we turn off the air conditioning and heat too."

Since then, this idea has grown in the industry. Some interesting examples:  (TODO add links for these if possible)
- **Philips** builds electric razors in the Netherlands with 128 robots and just nine human quality-assurance workers
- **Advanced chip fabs** running 300mm silicon wafers are almost entirely automated, with people there mainly to watch the process and repair machines when they break.
- **Xiaomi** runs an 860,000-square-foot plant in Changping, Beijing, capable of building 10 million smartphones a year across 11 fully automated production lines.
- **ASE Group**, the Taiwanese semiconductor packaging and testing giant, operates 56 lights-out factories.

## Project Dark Factory

Now, this term is re-emerging in the software AI revolution. I went to a Tokyo AI meetup around April and heard Leonard Lin from Shisa.AI speak about the dark factory concept coming to software. 
He mentioned two rules that a certain company is employing:
- Code must not be written by humans
- Code must not be reviewed by humans

Many companies see it as a means to replace people while others, including myself, see it as a way to empower people to do more. I'm calling **project dark factory** the implementation goal for product-centric companies to take on this concept of completely automating areas of their work.
